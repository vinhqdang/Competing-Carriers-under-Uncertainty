# Anticipated objections and honest answers

Section and table numbers refer to the compiled manuscript (`paper/main.pdf`, 69 pages; main text pp. 1-35; appendices A-G). Re-check the numbers after any further edit.

**1. "Every component rests on classical results; the novelty is incremental."**
Partly true, and the paper says so. Section 1 (contributions with novelty level) and the "Relation to prior work" paragraphs of Sections 4.1, 4.4 and 4.5 state what is classical (comonotone worst case and stop-loss decomposition; Pigou--Cournot tax; marginal-contribution tariffs; toll polyhedra) and what is new: the per-arc budget as a pricing resource and the completion-window dominance with its exactness proof (Theorems 2 and 4); the potential--welfare identity of the lumpy Cournot--congestion game with loss bounds, best responses as the separation step (Lemma 7, Theorem 10); the instance-free contraction rate (Theorem 18); and the explicit belief width (Theorem 27, Remark 29, which says the result is close to Liu--Oudjane--Wan). The paper does not claim a single breakthrough result. Its contribution is the combination in one tested pipeline.

**2. "The instances are stylised and many parameters are weak, so the numbers say nothing about real cities."**
Agreed for levels. Section 1 (Scope) and Section 6 ("Weak numbers", grades A-C) say the findings concern effects and orderings, not forecasts. Table F.17 lists the parameters and Table F.18 the sensitivity to the weakest ones; Section 9 (Model) lists the simplifications (all-or-nothing lots, linear congestion, equal market shares in seven cities). The grade-C parameters drive the size of the leader's gains, which is why Section 8.1 says the direction is better supported than the size.

**3. "The probability-box criterion is conservative and loses out of sample. Why use it?"**
The paper reports that it loses. Section 7.2 and Table 1: at every n up to 60 the p-box plan has a higher expected-cost regret (1.6-8.9%) than sample-average plans (0.16-0.27%); the cost comes from the DKW band, not from the unknown dependence. It buys a guaranteed upper bound, lower overtime probability and lower tail cost, and overtakes the sample-average plan under a common shock. On public instances BRL plans are worse than sample-average plans on held-out cost (Table F.25). Section 7.6 and Table 4 then show which game results depend on this criterion: levels do, orderings and policy directions do not.

**4. "BRL does not scale beyond about 40 customers and was not compared with function-label or resource-extension code."**
Both are stated. Section 7.1 and Table F.7 give where optimality was proven (30 zones for Cairo, London, New York; 40 for London; Solomon R101 with 25 customers) and where not (London 60 and 80, New Delhi 60, Augerat A-n32-k5). The label-count comparison (Table F.24) is against quantile-vector dominance and the same stop-loss comparison without the window; the window's advantage shrinks on real data. No code based on Irnich (2008), Dabia et al. (2013) or Ioachim et al. (1998) was run (Sections 4.1, 7.1, 9). The public-instance study is an emulation, not a reproduction of published branch-and-cut results.

**5. "DIP beating the grid is unfair: it searches a richer family and assumes optimistic equilibrium selection."**
Correct, and Section 7.5 says so. DIP uses one charge per zone, the grid two parameters; restricted to the grid's family DIP reaches a mean of 1.0 of the grid's gain, with a worst case of 0.50 (Mumbai, seed 0, a reduced grid). Table 3 therefore gives every method's gain as a share of the social optimum's gain. Selection is optimistic: under DIP's charges the equilibrium was unique in only 35% of the enumerated tiny games. DIP is no better than its social target (Proposition 11), which is a rounded optimum, not a proven one.

**6. "ARP's guarantee holds only for the convexified game; in the pure game it cycles."**
Yes. Theorems 17-19 are stated for the convexified game, and Theorem 19(b) is conditional on potential minimisation. Section 7.5 reports cycles and the clear miss (Table F.26, Figure 2, Table F.14), and that ARP is not better than DIP in solution quality, only in the information it needs and in cost. ARP was run in four cities (ten seeds in three).

**7. "The credal absorption theorem is a restatement of Liu--Oudjane--Wan, and its N-free terms do not bind on your instances."**
The paper states this itself (Remark 29, Section 1 contribution iv). The new content is the explicit belief box, exactness under E-admissibility, attainment of the N-1 term at N=2 (Proposition 34, Appendix A.1), the contrast with Gamma-maximin, and the complexity split between exposure-based and vehicle-count congestion (Theorem 31). On the city instances (N at most 6, d at least 84) only the (N-1) term applies, and Section 7.7 tests it (Table F.15) with the limitation that mixed equilibria were found only with two-plan supports.

**8. "Ten seeds in three cities, reduced grids in six, three seeds elsewhere: is the evidence strong enough?"**
Coverage is stated in Section 7 (protocol) and Table F.16. Means come with bootstrap intervals over seeds (Tables 2 and 3); quantities from reduced grids are marked as lower bounds. Findings that did not hold across seeds are reported as such (the ordering of the price of anarchy holds in 7 of 10 seeds; a congestion charge alone reaches 1.7% in 7 of 30 city-seeds). The imprecision sweeps and the criterion study use three seeds (Section 9).

**9. "The call asks for integration with learning methods, simulation, digital twins and real-time decisions. Where are they?"**
The paper does not contain them and does not claim them (Section 8.2, cover letter). Its fit is through the call's listed topics: robust optimisation under data uncertainty, urban logistics and vehicle routing, and public--private coordination. The data-driven elements are probability boxes built from observed travel-time days, instances built from open data, and ARP's observation-driven charge updates; ARP's post-observe-update loop is the unit a simulation or pilot would run. If the editors consider integration with learning or digital twins mandatory, the honest answer is that this is a limitation of scope.

**10. "Are the computations and the formal checks reliable?"**
All table numbers are generated from result files by `make_tables.py`, and the code and instances are public (Data and code availability). Not all oracle calls were proven optimal (Section 7 protocol, Section 9); every reported equilibrium was verified against an independent oracle with regret at most 1e-12 USD, with a positive guaranteed bound for a few profiles (Section 7.8). Several experiments (engine comparison, ZMC checks, theory tests, tiny games) still use price-and-branch, whose equilibria were not exact in some city-seeds (Sections 7.3, 9). Lean checks finite and algebraic cores only, and Appendix G lists exactly which; the build should be re-run before submission.
